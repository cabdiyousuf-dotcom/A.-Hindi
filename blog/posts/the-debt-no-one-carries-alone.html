The Debt No One Carries Alone

Long before I ever held a ministerial title, I learned how nomads govern risk. The word is mag, the traditional diya, or blood-compensation, system of Waqooyi Bari and the wider Somali interior.

Here is how it works. If a man causes harm, even by accident, the debt is not his to carry alone. It belongs to his mag-paying group, the extended kin bound together for exactly this purpose, sometimes numbering in the hundreds. Everyone pays. Everyone is watched. Everyone has a stake in keeping a hot-headed relative in check, because his mistake becomes the group's bill.

Call it what it is: social insurance, built by nomads long before the term existed in any funding proposal. It spreads risk across a community instead of leaving one family to absorb it. It gives victims a path to justice without a courthouse or a police post that might be three hundred kilometers away.

I watched elders apply this logic instinctively in negotiations I sat in as a minister, allocating responsibility across kin the way an insurer spreads it across policyholders.

We did not need to import this idea. We inherited it, and in the places state institutions still struggle to reach, it is still doing the job.
